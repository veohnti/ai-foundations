1 How would I evaluate the agents on each ticket, and why?

Ticket 101 · Bulldog tee Good

The team saw the real problem: we had to pay our own open invoice before we could promise the customer anything. Size S was at 0. The only apparel vendor, Bulldog Print Co, wouldn't ship while invoice 501 ($840, 3 days overdue) was open. Inventory caught that from get_vendor_restock_options, Accounting asked me to approve the invoice, and only after it was paid did Accounting prepare the $8 restock PO. That's the order a careful shop manager would follow, and the Cash tab shows it: −$840 first, then −$8.

The weak spot was the customer email. An earlier draft told Tauhid that "the restock has been paid and is being fulfilled by Bulldog Print Co". A customer doesn't need our vendor's name or our bills. I had the agent updated: Customer Service now has a "what customers may and may not hear" rule, and the MCP server refuses drafts that mention vendors, invoices, approvals, or payments. It's better now. In this run the guard refused 5 draft attempts on 101 for saying "approval", and the final draft (#11) just says size S is out of stock, the estimated arrival is September 5, and sizes M, L, and XL are available now.

Ticket 102 · Rent due Good, but over-built

This one was straightforward and the agents handled it cleanly. Facilities checked the notice against lease 1 ($2,400 due 2026-09-02), had Accounting prepare the rent request, I approved it, and checking dropped from $2,560 to $160. It had only 3 hand-offs, 22 tool calls, and about 64k tokens, the cheapest of the three.

We didn't really need a landlord email. Paying the rent was the outcome. Facilities still wrote two landlord drafts (#3 and #7), partly because my "resolved means a draft exists" rule pushed it to. The second draft was worse than none: after the payment, the system moves the lease's next due date to October 2, and Facilities read that as a disagreement with the landlord's "due September 2" notice. It flagged a discrepancy that doesn't exist. For rent, "paid and recorded" should count as resolved, with no draft.

Ticket 103 · Bulk hoodie discount Mixed

This was the most complicated ticket, and the agents at least got us to a clear answer: we can't do the full 20. Only 8 size-M hoodies were on hand. The 12-unit restock would cost $264 and checking only had $152, so I denied it. The final draft (#13) tells the Yale AI Club they can have 8 now and that the other 12 can't be supplied right now. Telling a customer "no" plainly is a fair resolution.

It never really dealt with the bulk discount the club asked for. Accounting ran check_margin nine times on 0–30% off, and the boss picked $46.40 (20% off). But the customer reply just says "$46.40 each". It never says the price is a bulk discount, never connects it to their request, and only applies it to the 8 we have. Nobody asked whether a group order of 8 still deserves a "bulk" price. No human signed off on giving away 20% either. The discount was handled as math, not as a decision, so I count it as an omission.

Across all three · efficiency Too many calls

Every agent called its tools again and again. Ticket 101 alone made 74 tool calls, including get_ticket_with_links 16 times, because every agent re-reads the ticket each time it's handed the job. Accounting even ran twice in parallel in the first pass, once for the boss and once for Inventory, and both produced the same answer. Ticket 103's first pass used 29 of its 30-request budget, so it nearly hit the cap. I think it could have been much more efficient:

  • pass the ticket facts along in the hand-off instead of re-reading them;
  • stop two teammates from asking Accounting the same question;
  • cache read-only lookups within a run.

That would roughly halve the 447k tokens this run cost.

3 What would have been simpler with one agent with tools, and why?

Facilities could just have been folded into Accounting. Ticket 102 shows why. Facilities did two things:

  • compared the notice with lease 1;
  • asked Accounting to prepare the rent request, because it isn't allowed to request payments itself.
Accounting already reads the lease through get_cash_and_obligations, which returns the rent amount and due date. One "money and obligations" agent could have verified the notice, requested the payment, and been done. That cuts out a hand-off, a duplicate cash lookup, and the second landlord draft that caused the false date "discrepancy".

More broadly, a single agent with the same 12 agent tools would have made several things simpler:

  • No re-reading: one agent reads ticket 101 once, not 16 times, and keeps the stock, invoice, and cash facts in one place.
  • No duplicate work: the two parallel Accounting runs on 101 couldn't have happened.
  • A smaller budget: the 30-request cap was never a risk for single-agent work. Ticket 103's 29-request first pass was mostly hand-off overhead.
  • Easier debugging and cheaper: one feed to read instead of a 14-chip web, and roughly half the tokens.

What I'd keep separate: Customer Service. Having a dedicated writer behind a privacy guard is what kept vendor names and "we paid our invoice" out of Tauhid's email. With one agent that knows everything, that's exactly the kind of internal detail that leaks.

4 Three new problems Campus Customs could handle with these agents and tools

(a) "How much does this cost?"

A student asks the price of the Yale Cap or the Classic Bulldog Tee. check_stock_and_price returns the list price ($22 cap, $28 tee) and stock, and Customer Service can draft a short reply. For example: "The Yale Cap is $22 and we have plenty in stock (40)". The privacy guard keeps our $6 unit cost out of it. This is ticket 101's flow without the restock problem, and it's simple enough for one or two tool calls.

(b) "What are our margins?"

This is exactly what Accounting did on ticket 103. check_margin showed $46.40 leaves $24.40 per hoodie (52.6%), and list price leaves $36. The same tool can answer margins for any SKU we sell: the tee ($28 vs $8 cost), the cap ($22 vs $6), and the mug ($14 vs $3.50). It can also tell the manager which item earns the most per sale.

(c) "How many are left of a different item?"

check_stock_and_price already returns every size of a SKU, which is how Customer Service could offer Tauhid sizes M (5), L (3), and XL (2). The same lookup works across SKUs. For example: "We have 14 hoodies in L, but the Crest Mug is sold out." Inventory can then use get_vendor_restock_options to say mugs come from Elm City Gifts in about 3 days, with no open invoice blocking them.

5 Three problems the agents couldn't handle, why, and the tools they'd need

(a) Bulk discounts and pricing strategy

Why they can't: ticket 103 proves it. The agents can calculate a margin, but they have nothing to decide with. The database has one list price and one cost per SKU: no discount policy, no quantity tiers, no sales history, no demand. So the boss picked 20% off by judgment, and nobody asked whether 8 hoodies even qualify as "bulk". Pricing also isn't on the human approval list, so a model gave away $92.80 of margin on its own.

Tools I'd need:

  • a pricing_policy table with tiers (e.g. 10+ units 10% off, 20+ units 15%) and a floor;
  • a recommend_price(sku, qty) tool that maximizes profit within that policy, given margins and stock;
  • a discount approval kind, so any price below list goes to me like a payment does.

(b) Suggesting a similar item (not just another size)

Why they can't: the catalog only has SKU, name, and size. Nothing says the Classic Bulldog Tee and the Basic Hoodie are both Yale apparel, or what color or style anything is. So the best Customer Service could do for Tauhid was "try size M, L, or XL". It couldn't suggest a different shirt that would still make him happy. The agents also aren't allowed to invent product facts, so they shouldn't guess.

Tools I'd need:

  • product attributes (category, color, style, material) in the database;
  • a find_similar_items(sku) comparison tool that ranks in-stock alternatives across SKUs;
  • ideally the margin of each alternative, so we suggest ones that are also good for the shop.

(c) "Where's my order?" after a customer has already ordered

Why they can't: there's no orders or shipments table. The run "approved" a tee PO, but the database doesn't record when it was placed, whether Bulldog Print Co shipped it, or when it reached the shop. Inventory only goes up when someone updates it by hand. Today's arrival estimate is just "desk date + lead days" (September 5). If Tauhid asks on September 7 where his tee is, the agents can only repeat the old estimate. The QuickShip CT courier (1-day lead) sits in the vendor table, but no tool can use it.

Tools I'd need:

  • an orders table (customer, item, date placed, status);
  • a shipments table linked to POs;
  • a track_order(order_id) tool that recalculates the arrival from the date the order was placed plus the vendor's lead time;
  • a tool to mark goods received, so stock updates when the restock arrives.
